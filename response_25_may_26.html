My admittedly rather extreme position is that generative AI should not be used to create anything that isn't a throwaway product. The capability of the AI tool to do a good job is not very relevant as long as humans have to own, reason about, and maintain the products.

I could write a book about this, but in short, responsibility in engineering is always going to fall on a human, not a tool. So, we need our humans to be as skilled and knowledgeable as possible. Skills are developed by learning how to do things and then doing them over and over many times. Knowledge is reinforced by applying it, by encountering problems where it's relevant and digging it out of memory to use it.

Generative AI doesn't help someone develop skills or knowledge. It just provides a shortcut to an outcome.

This is obviously disastrous for students, and that's where a lot of attention is going. But I think AI use is just as bad for trained professionals. Skills that aren't used are lost, and AI use takes it a step further by not just neglecting the skills, but rewiring the brain to approach the problem in a new way. When someone is using an AI, he's functioning as a supervisor or manager, not an engineer. "I'm going to do X" becomes "tell [worker] to do X for me". When every engineer is a manager, who's left to care about the details?

The unspoken assumption of the AI push is that we've entered some kind of post-knowledge society, where it doesn't matter if people actually understand anything because they can just ask the AI. Like how it doesn't matter if people are good at math, because they always have a calculator in their pocket... except the calculator is only as useful as the operator's math knowledge allows it to be. It doesn't matter how powerful the calculator is; it doesn't bestow knowledge by simply existing.

I'm not against tools in general. I think tools that mitigate the human tendency toward error in tedious, repetitive tasks are very good things. I just think tools should be narrowly scoped and intentionally designed, which is the exact opposite of generative AI.

As a postscript I'll tie all this into what you said about comments and code quality. Ultimately, the code itself is the source of truth no matter who wrote it. Comments can't be trusted because they easily go stale. They can be helpful as a starting point, but it's still up to the maintainer to verify them. Who's better equipped to do that, the guy who writes code by hand or the guy who asks an AI to do it for him?
